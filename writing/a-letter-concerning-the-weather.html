A letter concerning the weather

3 October 2025

Pétur Jónsson of Fell Farm had been writing letters to the Meteorological Office in Reykjavík for eleven years, and in all that time he had received exactly one reply, which thanked him for his interest and informed him that his observations, while appreciated, could not be incorporated into the national forecast as his thermometer had not been officially calibrated. Pétur had written back to explain that his thermometer was a gift from his uncle, who had been to Denmark and was therefore a man of science, but this letter went unanswered, as did all subsequent ones.

The letters were written on Sundays, after church, which Pétur did not attend. He had nothing against God but felt that their relationship, like all the best relationships, benefited from a certain distance. Instead he sat at the kitchen table with a cup of coffee and a sheet of paper and composed his weekly report. The reports were precise and, in their way, beautiful. He recorded the temperature at dawn and at noon and at the hour when the last light left the mountain. He noted the direction of the wind using a system of his own devising, which replaced the cardinal points with the names of neighbours: a north wind was a Guðmundur, because Guðmundur lived to the north and was similarly cold and persistent; a south wind was a Hildur, for reasons Pétur kept to himself.

He recorded rainfall by placing a tin bucket outside the front door and measuring its contents with a ruler. He recorded snowfall by the same method, though in winter the bucket often blew away, and he was obliged to estimate, which he did with the confidence of a man who has looked at snow for sixty years and considers himself, on this subject at least, an authority.

His wife, Guðrún, who had been dead for three years, had once asked him why he continued to write the letters when it was clear that no one in Reykjavík read them. Pétur had considered this question for most of an afternoon before answering that the weather happened whether or not anyone wrote it down, but that writing it down was a way of paying attention, and paying attention was the only thing a person could do that was not, in the end, a waste of time.

Guðrún had said that this was either very wise or very foolish, and that in her experience the two were often the same thing, and then she had gone to feed the chickens, and they had not discussed it again.

After Guðrún died, Pétur continued the letters. He also continued to set two places at the table and to say good morning to the empty chair, not because he believed she was there but because the habit was older than the grief, and he saw no reason why death should get to decide which of his habits he kept.

On the Sunday in question — which was the fourteenth of October, a day of low cloud and intermittent rain, with a mild Hildur and temperatures between four and seven degrees — Pétur finished his letter and folded it into an envelope and addressed it, as always, to The Director, Meteorological Office, Reykjavík. He walked to the postbox at the end of the road, which was a distance of one kilometre and which took him past the church, where he raised his hat, and past Guðmundur's farm, where he did not.

He posted the letter. He stood for a moment in the light rain. The mountain was invisible behind the cloud, but he knew it was there, as he knew the sea was beyond it, and beyond the sea, Reykjavík, where a young person in an office would perhaps hold his letter for a moment before placing it, unread, in a drawer or a bin. This did not trouble him. The weather had been recorded. The attention had been paid. He turned and walked home, and the rain, which did not know his name, fell on him with the same tenderness it fell on everything.
