Tools that think with you

24 January 2026

There is a difference between a tool that does work for you and a tool that thinks alongside you. The first kind saves time. The second kind changes what you're able to think.

A spreadsheet is the classic example. Before spreadsheets, financial modelling meant rows of pencilled numbers, an eraser worn to nothing, and the quiet dread of discovering an error in row forty-seven. The spreadsheet didn't just speed this up. It made it possible to ask "what if?" — to change one assumption and watch a hundred consequences ripple through. The tool didn't do the thinking. It created the conditions for a different kind of thought.

Most software today optimises for the first category. It automates. It completes. It removes friction. And removing friction is good, up to a point. But friction is sometimes where understanding lives. When you struggle to phrase a query, you're clarifying what you actually want to know. When you reorganise your notes by hand, you notice connections you'd otherwise miss.

The tools I keep returning to — a plain text editor, a terminal, a notebook with a pen — share a quality that's hard to name. They are low-resolution enough that I have to supply the structure. They don't suggest. They wait. And in that waiting, they leave room for me to surprise myself.

This isn't an argument against intelligence in tools. It's an argument for a specific kind of intelligence: the kind that amplifies rather than replaces. A tool that thinks with you might surface a pattern you hadn't noticed, or hold a thread of context while you explore a tangent. But it shouldn't collapse the space of possibilities into a single suggestion. The moment a tool becomes an oracle, you stop being a thinker and start being a reviewer.

I've been paying attention to how this plays out in practice. The best collaborative moments — with people or with software — have a quality of genuine back-and-forth. You propose something rough. The other side refracts it, adds a dimension you hadn't considered. You push back. Something emerges that neither of you would have found alone.

The worst moments are when one side is simply executing the other's instructions. It doesn't matter which direction the instructions flow. Whether you're dictating to a tool or deferring to one, the result is the same: a narrowing.

What I want from a tool is not that it be smart. I want it to make me smarter. There's a world of difference.
