The fisherman's wife

15 November 2025

The woman stood at the window and counted the waves. This was not a habit born of madness, though her sister-in-law, who lived in the capital and knew about such things, had once suggested as much during an Easter visit. It was simply what there was to do when the boats were out and the radio had said nothing and the coffee was already cold in the pot.

She counted seven waves between each of the good ones. The good ones were those that broke cleanly against the rock below the church, sending a plume of white spray into the air that, if the light was right, carried a brief and unreliable rainbow. Her husband had told her once that the seventh wave was always the largest. This was a lie, or at best an approximation, but she had found it useful in the way that all superstitions are useful: it gave shape to what would otherwise be shapeless time.

The house was of the old kind, built from concrete and corrugated iron, painted the colour of rust because it had originally been painted red and time had, as it does in that part of the world, improved upon the intention. There were two rooms downstairs and one upstairs, which was called the good room, though nothing particularly good had ever happened in it. The good room contained a writing desk that no one wrote at, a shelf of books that no one read, and a photograph of the woman's mother-in-law, who had died in 1987 and whose expression in the photograph suggested she had foreseen this and considered it an inconvenience.

At half past three, the woman put on her coat and walked to the harbour. The coat was too large for her. It had belonged to her husband before it had belonged to her, and before that it had belonged to his father, and before that it had perhaps belonged to the sea itself, for it smelled of salt and diesel and something older than either. She wore it not for warmth but for company.

The harbour was empty, which was its usual condition. There was a pier, and on the pier there was a bollard, and tied to the bollard there was a rope that led to nothing, its frayed end trailing in the water like a question no one had thought to finish asking. The woman sat on the concrete ledge and looked out at the horizon, which was the same grey as the sky above it and the sea below it, so that the world appeared to be a single substance, undifferentiated and vast.

She did not worry. Worry was for people who believed that their feelings had some bearing on events. The woman had lived long enough by the sea to know that it did not care what you felt about it. The boats would come back or they would not. The fish would be plenty or they would be few. The wind would turn or it would hold. None of this had anything to do with the small, warm, persistent thing that lived in her chest and that she would not have called love, because that word had been ruined by the radio.

At a quarter to five, the first boat appeared. It was not her husband's boat. It was Sigurður's boat, which was painted blue and sat low in the water, meaning the catch had been good. She watched it come in without expression. Then the second boat. Then the third.

Her husband's boat was the fourth. It was painted no colour in particular. He raised his hand as he passed the breakwater, and she raised hers, and this was all they had ever needed.

She walked home ahead of him, because the coffee would need reheating and there was bread to cut, and because some things are better said by being done than by being spoken. The waves were still counting themselves against the rock below the church. She did not look back. There was no need. The seventh wave broke, and then the counting began again.
